## There was a Gridcoin before this one

The chain you are browsing, Gridcoin **Research**, opens at [block 0 in October 2014](/block/0). It was not the first Gridcoin. On **October 16, 2013**, a pseudonymous developer who went by **Rob Halförd** announced a coin on the Bitcointalk forum with a one-line pitch that still sums the whole project up: *why mine when you can research?*

That was Gridcoin Classic ([^Gridcoin Classic — gridcoin.us wiki](https://gridcoin.us/wiki/gridcoin-classic.html)). Different chain, different codebase, different block history. This explorer does not index it and never will, because Classic is not the chain it talks to. The page exists so the story starts where the story actually starts.

The name is a small piece of community folklore worth keeping. "Rob Halförd" is not a real person. The handle is a nod to Rob Halford of Judas Priest, reportedly because an early profile picture looked the part. The founder stayed pseudonymous for years, which matters more than it sounds, and we will come back to it.

## What Classic actually ran

Classic was a scrypt **proof-of-work** coin with a proof-of-stake layer bolted on, forked from the Bitcoin and Peercoin lineage. The novel part was the reward: instead of paying purely for hashing, it tried to pay for [BOINC](https://boinc.berkeley.edu/) science. The ambition was right. The implementation was not.

The early client measured a machine's BOINC contribution by reading CPU utility more or less directly, and that was trivially gameable. You did not have to do the science to look like you had. For a coin whose entire reason to exist was "the work is real," that was not a bug you patch around. It was a reason to start over.

## The rebuild, decided in public

By February 2014 Halförd was openly floating a redesign on the forums. The plan: drop the gameable CPU-reading scheme, anchor researcher identity to BOINC itself, and lean on staking rather than hashing for chain security. Then came roughly seven months of testnet before the new chain went live. Eight months, start to finish, spent rebuilding a coin's core because version one could be cheated. That is the founding instinct of the project, and it shows up again in every later hard fork in this archive.

## How Classic ended

Classic did not get switched off. It was wound down on a schedule, running next to Research while holders moved across:

- **2014-10-12** — the conversion window opens. Classic balances migrate to Research at **1 Classic to 10 Research**, by burning to an address with no recoverable key.
- **2015-04-26** — the window closes after about six months. Whatever was never converted, plus accrued interest, rolls into a community foundation fund.

During that window the two coins traded under different tickers: Classic became **GRCX**, Research kept **GRC**. Confirmation requirements scaled with the size of the burn, from 10 confirmations for small amounts up to 300 for the largest, to keep the one-way exchange honest.

There is a loose end in that story. The unconverted coins, a pool that wiki records put around 340 million GRC, sat in an address one anonymous person controlled. It stayed that way until 2019, when it was finally moved into a 2-of-3 community multisig ([^Gridcoin foundation fund — gridcoin.us wiki](https://gridcoin.us/wiki/foundation.html)). For roughly four years a single pseudonymous individual sat on the project's treasury and, as far as the public record shows, simply did not touch it.

## Why it belongs in the chronicle

If you came here for 2013 block data, the section below is empty, and it should be: Classic is not on this chain. What carried forward was everything that was not a block. The compute-for-science thesis, the BOINC playbook, the founder, the early community, and the hard lesson that the science has to be unfakeable. [The 2014 chain](/blocks/2014) inherited the idea and rebuilt the machine to actually deliver it.
