## A year with no fork

Some years on this chain are turning points. 2016 wasn't one. **{{stat:total_blocks}} blocks**, **{{stat:total_txs}} transactions**, **{{stat:grc_moved}} GRC** moved, **{{stat:grc_minted}} GRC** minted, and no consensus change worth a landmark. The chain kept producing a block every ninety seconds while the interesting things happened elsewhere.

Most of a blockchain's life looks like this. Read this archive top to bottom and Gridcoin looks like a string of dramatic forks. Mostly it's years like 2016, where the protocol stays put and the project argues with itself about what it is.

The release history is thin too. 3.5.6.3, from April 23, is the oldest tagged release in the repository. 3.5.8.0 followed in October, and 3.5.8.2 on November 1 was the year's only mandatory upgrade.

## The superblock that paid three projects

The neural network that built the daily superblocks [since 2015](/blocks/2015) worked most days, and 2016 showed what happened when it didn't. On August 19, a superblock went out listing only three BOINC projects: Asteroids@home, Atlas@home and BURP. Every researcher on those projects got roughly ten times their usual magnitude for a day, and everyone else got nothing ([^Three-project superblock, Gridcoin-Research issue #115](https://github.com/gridcoin-community/Gridcoin-Research/issues/115)). It's the first documented superblock failure, and the issue opened a week later is an early sign of the fragility that blew up in 2017.

(As on the [2015 page](/blocks/2015), the explorer shows zero superblocks for this year because the current wallet doesn't validate superblocks below its grandfather height. They happened. The explorer just can't count them.)

## Where the community lived

From late summer, the community started moving its conversations to Steemit. Community hangouts were organised there by September, and release announcements followed. That's why so much of Gridcoin's history from 2016 to 2020, including most release posts, sits on Steemit and later Hive instead of a forum or mailing list.

## The flaw nobody had announced

On September 14, 2016, a group of security researchers at Ruhr-University Bochum emailed the developers about serious problems ([^"Breaking and Fixing Gridcoin," USENIX WOOT'17](https://www.usenix.org/conference/woot17/workshop-program/presentation/grothe)). The public didn't hear about it. They had found two things. First, the obfuscation protecting researchers' BOINC email addresses on chain could be reversed, and they recovered 2,709 of them. Second, an attacker could take a researcher's BOINC work and collect the rewards for it.

The developers replied the next day and suggested dropping the beacon signature check. The researchers rebutted that the same day and heard nothing back, and an October 31 follow-up also went unanswered. The story doesn't end in 2016. It ends, messily, [in 2017](/blocks/2017), on a conference stage.

2016 ended where it began: the chain on stable footing, a pile of unsolved security and fairness problems on the table, and a fragile snapshot system that had just shown how badly it could fail. All of it came due the next year.
